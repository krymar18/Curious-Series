Using AI Without Making Enemies

AI etiquette at work is less about being polite to the tool and more about being responsible to the people affected by your AI-assisted work. Read each rule, check your own behavior against it, and use it before applying AI to client or colleague-facing work.

The Real Tells Human or AI?

Five rules for working with AI

1Protect sensitive information

AI providers use many data protections, but they are not perfect. Because these tools are still new and changing quickly, the risk can be higher than in standard Microsoft workflows. For example, in July 2026, shared Claude chats were reportedly searchable through Google, exposing sensitive client information (only applies to non-enterprise accounts). That does not mean AI should never be used, but it does mean we need to weigh the business value against the data risk.

To understand Ampersand's AI risk tolerance, start with the AI policy. If the policy does not clearly cover the data you are using, ask your AI Steering Committee lead for guidance. Avoid putting HR confidential information, business secrets, or client-restricted data into any chatbot unless it has first been anonymized and reviewed by the AI Steering Committee. Also remember that deleting a chat may not delete every stored or archived copy, and vendor records may still be available through legal or law-enforcement requests.

2Disclose AI use when it affects trust

AI can speed up work, but it can also hallucinate, miss context, and introduce errors. For internal handoffs, state when AI was used and which part of the process it supported, especially if a colleague will rely on the output. Although some people worry that disclosure can make the work seem less professional, many teams now use AI regularly. Clear disclosure gives reviewers the context they need to catch mistakes, question assumptions, improve the final result, and build shared AI literacy.

This guidance applies to internal communication only. For external work, follow the AI or any other relevant policy. If unclear, consult the AI Steering Committee.

Optional reading
Atlassian — "New research shows honesty about AI use at work is backfiring"

3Verify before you hand it off

Good AI work is about 40% understanding the problem, 10% chatting with the tool, and 50% validating the result. Whoever uses AI is still responsible for checking the output, even if a few small errors may slip through. Always include the review time in your estimate and make the work as accurate as possible before handing it off.

Unchecked AI erodes trust within the team and makes collaboration harder. If circumstances benefit from sharing unchecked work, make it explicit during the handoff.

Optional reading
Harvard Business School Institute for Business in Global Society — "AI won't make the call: Why human judgment still drives innovation"

4Do not outsource cleanup to colleagues

AI has made it easy to create long "working drafts". A 20-page document that takes five minutes to generate can still take someone else 40 minutes to read, question, and clean up. Under most circumstances, AI will include a lot of filler in any non-generic text, make sure to remove it. Making someone read five 20-page documents in one day will likely feel dismissive and will hurt the relationship and productivity.

Optional reading

5Cherish and protect human relationships

AI can help draft routine messages, but use judgment before applying it to inter-personal communication. A thank-you note, birthday message, or response to someone's help can feel hollow if it sounds generic or overproduced. As AI becomes part of daily work, we need to be intentional about preserving the human relationships we have spent years building.

The Real Tells

What actually separates AI writing from human writing — and what doesn't.

A number of so-called "tells" are frequently repeated to the point of becoming common folk wisdom, such as the use of em dashes, semicolons, and writing that sounds excessively polished. On their own, none of these serve as proof that AI was used; em dashes, in fact, appeared long before the development of AI and are widely used in the fields of law, journalism, and academia.

While AI is still hard to detect reliably, certain signs can still cause doubt, for example, those relating to unusually even sentence structure or excessively patterned phrasing. In a business context, we don't care as much whether something sounds produced by AI, but rather how it impacts our clients' and colleagues' perception of us. AI-sounding communication can at times damage trust or give the impression that minimal effort has been made.

Strong tendencies in AI writing tend to appear in the form of patterns rather than as individual markers: unusually even sentence length, the use of consistently well-polished but general phrasing, repeated explanatory structure, vague transitions, overuse of certain stylistic words, and a style that seems smooth yet contains no specific context or personal details.

Yet they remain nothing more than indications and not concrete evidence. While a group of signs may warrant a more detailed examination, they cannot prove that AI was used, particularly since highly skilled human writing can exhibit many of the same characteristics.

Pair 1 — Client email: billing discrepancy

Click the text you think is AI-written

"Hi Maria, thanks for flagging this. I checked the March invoice against the trafficking log — the $4,200 discrepancy is from spot #114837 running in the wrong daypart for six days before ops caught it. I've adjusted the invoice and it should hit your portal by Friday. Sorry for the back and forth on this one."

"Hi Maria, thank you for reaching out regarding this matter. After a comprehensive review of the March invoice, we identified a discrepancy that has now been thoroughly addressed and resolved. Our team is committed to accuracy and transparency, and we appreciate your patience as we worked to rectify this issue."

Answer: The AI version never names the number, the spot, or the cause — it's confident and polished with nothing to check. The human version gives a figure, a spot number, a reason, and a date, and ends on an actual apology instead of a closing formula.

Pair 2 • Internal recap: campaign performance meeting

Click the text you think is AI-written

"Recapping the QBR call: national auto client is pacing 12% behind on the CTV bundle, mostly a Q1 inventory issue on our end, not their targeting. Sales is proposing a makegood in Q2. Data flagged that the attribution partner switch might explain part of the gap too; need to confirm by next week."

"Great call today! Key takeaways: the campaign is performing well overall, though there are some areas for improvement. The team discussed various strategies to optimize performance and drive better results moving forward. Everyone agreed to reconvene and continue monitoring progress closely."

Answer: The AI version has no number, no client name, no owner, and no next step beyond "monitoring." The human version names the client type, the exact percentage, the cause, the proposed fix, and an open question with a deadline.

Pair 3 • Announcement post: product update

Click the text you think is AI-written

"We just turned on cross-screen deduplication for our set-top box audiences. Small feature, big deal for anyone tired of counting the same household twice across linear and CTV. More on this soon."

"We are thrilled to announce a groundbreaking new feature that will revolutionize the way our clients approach cross-screen measurement. This innovative solution empowers advertisers to unlock unprecedented insights and drive meaningful results."

Answer: The AI version is built almost entirely from the overused-word list (groundbreaking, revolutionize, innovative, empowers, unprecedented, meaningful); it never names an actual feature. The human version names the specific feature and the specific problem it solves, then stops.

How to Keep Building Your AI Skills

The best way to build AI skill is to practice on real work in small, repeatable steps: start with simple tasks, add context as the work gets bigger, save prompts that work, and review AI output before trusting it.

1

Start small

Use AI first on one clear task, such as rewriting one paragraph, summarizing one thread, or turning rough notes into bullets. If the result is unpredictable, shrink the task before making the prompt longer.

2

Brief the AI like a teammate

For larger tasks, write the prompt like a mini-brief: background, goal, audience, source material, format, tone, constraints, and what a good answer should include.

3

Practice before the deadline

Use low-stakes work you already understand, such as an old recap, a rough email, or a training outline. Practice works best when you know what a good answer should look like and have time to compare.

4

Save what works

Keep prompts for repeatable work like meeting summaries, client emails, document reviews, spreadsheet checks, and training handouts. Save the prompt changes that improved the result, not just the final output.

5

Review before you rely on it

Treat AI output like work from a fast junior teammate. Check names, numbers, dates, claims, tone, and whether the answer skipped an important constraint.
