Copilot in Outlook

A practical reference for every major Copilot feature inside Microsoft Outlook — reading, drafting, scheduling, and the proactive features most people never discover.

Objective

Use Copilot to process email faster, draft more effectively, and prepare for meetings without the manual back-and-forth — without losing your voice in the messages that matter.

Key idea

Outlook is where Copilot has the broadest view of your work: email history, calendar, and meeting context simultaneously. That cross-signal intelligence is its core value — and its most important risk to understand.

The most important Outlook setting

For inbox-wide queries and meeting prep to work well, your inbox needs to reflect how you actually communicate. Decisions made on Teams calls and referenced only obliquely in email will be missed. The quality of what Copilot returns reflects your communication habits.

Features

Three categories — reading, writing, and scheduling. Each has a different reliability profile and a different ideal workflow.

Reading Assistance — Thread Summary, Action Items & Inbox Queries

The most reliable category. Copilot reads email threads and your inbox to surface what matters without you having to read everything.

Thread Summary M365 Copilot
Excellent at
  • Quickly catching up on long threads without reading every reply
  • Extracting the core issue and open questions before a meeting
  • Processing high-volume inboxes faster by reading summaries first
Better avoided for
  • Implied commitments or tone that only the full thread conveys
  • Forwarding the summary without reading it — misattribution is the most common error
  • Threads where subtext, relationship history, or tone matters more than stated content
Thread summary prompt

Summarise this thread in three bullet points: the main issue, what has been agreed so far, and what is still outstanding or unresolved.

Action item extraction

Extract all action items from this thread. For each: the task, the owner, and any deadline mentioned. Format as a numbered list.

Exercise — Thread Summary in Practice
  1. Open a thread with 10+ replies you haven't read yet.
  2. Open the Copilot summary card. Read it before reading the thread.
  3. Now read the thread. What did the summary get right? What did it miss or misattribute?
  4. Reflection: For which types of email would you trust the summary enough to act on it directly?
Inbox-Wide Queries M365 Copilot
Excellent at
  • Surfacing pending items from a specific person or project across many threads
  • End-of-week accountability sweeps across your whole inbox
  • Finding decisions or commitments you made that you need to follow up on
Better avoided for
  • Implied commitments — Copilot misses 'I'll look into it' type language
  • Old content: results degrade significantly beyond 3–4 weeks
  • Situations where key decisions happened on Teams or in meetings, not email
Inbox-wide pending items

What emails from [person or company] in the last 30 days still need a response from me?

Today's inbox prioritisation

Prioritise my unread emails today: (1) needs immediate action, (2) reply this week, (3) FYI only. For each action item, tell me what is needed and by when.

Writing Assistance — Drafting, Coaching & Reply Assist

The highest daily-use category. Copilot generates, refines, and coaches email drafts — but the judgment about what to say always stays with you.

Draft from Brief M365 Copilot
Excellent at
  • Routine communications where speed matters more than personal voice: scheduling, updates, confirmations
  • Breaking out of blank-page paralysis on longer, structured emails
  • Generating across multiple tone options to find the right register quickly
Better avoided for
  • Sensitive messages where your personal relationship and judgment matter — write it yourself and use coaching instead
  • Messages where the recipient would expect your voice specifically
  • Sending without personalising — an obviously AI-drafted email to the wrong person is worse than no email
Draft from brief

Draft an email to [name] about [topic]. Key points: [1], [2], [3]. Tone: professional but not stiff. Length: concise.

Exercise — Draft vs Coach Comparison
  1. Think of a real email you need to send this week.
  2. Use Draft with Copilot. Write a plain-language brief — not the email itself. Accept the draft.
  3. Discard it. Write the email yourself. Run Coaching by Copilot on your version.
  4. Compare: which is better? Where did the generated draft fail? What did coaching catch?
  5. Reflection: For this type of email, which workflow saved more time?
Coaching Mode M365 Copilot
Excellent at
  • Sensitive messages: client escalations, performance feedback, difficult internal communications
  • Any email where you would hesitate before sending — write it yourself first, then coach
  • Catching unintended tone, buried asks, or passive-aggressive phrasing you didn’t notice
Better avoided for
  • Emails under 100 characters — Copilot needs enough text to analyse
  • Messages where you want Copilot to replace your judgment, not improve your delivery
  • Treating coaching suggestions as mandatory — they are inputs, not corrections
Coaching refinement

Review my draft and suggest improvements for tone, clarity, and conciseness. I want it to sound direct but not abrupt. Flag anything that could land badly.

Reply Assist M365 Copilot
Excellent at
  • Quick contextual replies where the thread gives Copilot enough to work with
  • Inline editing of your own draft: shorter, different tone, rephrase a specific paragraph
  • Mobile email processing — the vibe writing experience is particularly fluid on the Outlook app
Better avoided for
  • Replies requiring specific knowledge only you have — Copilot writes from the thread, not from your history
  • Sensitive threads where a wrong AI reply could damage a relationship
Rewrite to be more direct

Rewrite my draft reply to be more direct. Lead with the answer, not the context. Cut it by 30%. Keep the professional tone.

Scheduling — Meeting from Thread, Calendar Prep & Scheduled Prompts

The most powerful scheduling category in M365. Meeting prep and scheduled prompts are both underused and genuinely valuable.

Meeting from Thread & Calendar Prep M365 Copilot
Excellent at
  • Creating a meeting invite directly from a long email thread — Copilot drafts the agenda from the open questions
  • Meeting prep briefings before recurring client calls or senior leadership check-ins
  • Synthesising context from emails, Teams recaps, and shared files before a meeting
Better avoided for
  • Complex scheduling across external attendees — availability lookups break outside the tenant
  • Executing reschedule actions without reviewing — never let conflict resolution send emails automatically
  • Assuming prep briefings are complete — they depend on transcribed Teams history being available
Meeting prep briefing

Prepare me for my meeting with [name/team] on [date]. Summarise: what we last discussed, any open items I committed to, and what they are likely to want to address based on recent emails.

Scheduled Prompts M365 Copilot
Excellent at
  • Monday morning week-start briefings — prevents inbox surprises before they compound
  • Friday accountability sweeps across commitments made during the week
  • Client-specific monitoring: surface anything from a key account that needs your attention
Better avoided for
  • Vague scheduled prompts — they produce vague summaries. Write them with specific scope and constraints
  • Treating the output as a replacement for your own inbox check — it’s a supplement, not a substitute
Monday morning briefing (set as scheduled prompt)

Review this week's emails. Flag anything still needing a reply, summarise my key commitments, and list any meetings today I should prepare for.

Friday accountability sweep (set as scheduled prompt)

List all emails where I committed to something this week and have not yet replied or followed up. Include the original email subject, the commitment, and the recipient.

Ampersand Spotlight — Using This Responsibly

Copilot drafts in your name and reads your entire inbox. Both carry real implications for how you communicate and what gets surfaced.

AI-drafted emails carry your name

When Copilot drafts an email, the sender line says it came from you. For routine coordination this is fine. For sensitive messages — client complaints, personal acknowledgments, difficult conversations — the recipient may reasonably expect your voice. Ask yourself: if they knew this was AI-drafted, would it change how they receive it?

Inbox-wide queries surface everything

Copilot can see your entire inbox, including conversations you may have forgotten — HR threads, salary discussions, legal communications, personal messages. AI-assisted search makes previously buried content trivially findable. Be deliberate about what you ask it to surface.

Scheduling autonomy is a trust decision

Copilot’s scheduling features can send invitations and reschedule notifications on your behalf. Never let conflict resolution execute changes without reviewing the proposed actions. A rescheduling email sent to an external client before you see it is very difficult to recover from.

Scheduled prompts are standing instructions

A scheduled prompt is an instruction you set once that Copilot executes automatically on a recurring basis. Write specific, constrained prompts. Vague standing instructions produce vague weekly summaries that provide no value — or worse, surface incomplete information that gives false confidence.

Conclusion & Reflections

Take a moment to reflect — it’s what makes the learning stick.

Key Takeaways
  • Thread summary is the most reliable feature — use it as your default for long threads before you read or reply.
  • Draft from brief works best with a detailed prompt. The more specific the brief, the better the draft.
  • Coaching mode is underused. For high-stakes messages, write it yourself and let Copilot improve the delivery.
  • Meeting prep quality depends on transcribed Teams history. Better meetings produce better prep briefings.
  • Scheduled prompts are set-it-and-forget-it only if you write specific prompts. Vague prompts produce vague outputs.
Reflection
  • Think about where you spend the most time in email. Which of the three categories — reading, drafting, scheduling — would free up the most time?
  • For which types of email would you never use Copilot to generate? What makes those different?
  • If Copilot briefed you before every meeting, what would you still need to prepare manually — and why?
  • Have you ever sent an email and later regretted the tone? Would coaching mode have caught it?
