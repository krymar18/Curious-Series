Working with Language

Objective

Understand how AI reads and writes text, where each approach is most useful, and how to manage the tradeoff between speed and quality of output.

Rationale

Of everything AI can do, language processing will likely have the biggest impact on your day-to-day work. This section covers how to use it effectively and how to manage the tradeoff between generating content quickly and preserving its quality.

How AI Reads and Writes

AI generates language using probability by predicting the next word based on all surrounding words. Because humans naturally use similar words when discussing similar topics, this produces output that mostly accounts for the meaning without ever understanding it directly. We do not understand how this statistical similarity translates into something that resembles coherent reasoning; it is more something we observe than explain. But with due caution we can use such "reasoning" to our advantage.

Reading text

Human speech, regardless of language or culture, uses consistent structural patterns to separate thoughts, signal transitions, and indicate emphasis. LLMs exploit this statistical regularity to identify those patterns and isolate individual units of meaning, as well as signals of how much weight the writer places on them (repetition, position, explicit markers like "importantly" or "in summary"). This is what makes summarization and question-answering over large texts with surprising precision possible.

↗ Where this is most useful
  • Summarising a long email thread
  • Getting the key points from a report or contract
  • Extracting action items and owners from a meeting transcript
  • Pulling specific fields from multiple similar documents into one table
  • Producing a catch-up brief for a colleague who missed a meeting
  • Condensing a lengthy policy into a plain-language quick-reference

What AI handles vs. what you must own

AI
  • Compressing large volumes of text to the key points
  • Extracting specific elements by type (decisions, actions, dates, figures)
  • Transcribing spoken content
Human
  • Deciding whether the summary captures the right emphasis for your purpose
  • Verifying action items are attributed to the right person before acting
  • Reading the summary critically — AI cannot capture tone, intent, or what was left unsaid

Limitations and how to address them

AI summarizes based on what summaries typically look like — not based on what matters to you.
How to address it
Tell AI what you care about before it summarises. "Summarise this, focusing on anything related to pricing and timelines" produces a more useful output than "summarise this".
AI can hallucinate when extracting structured information. Action items can be misattributed — who said what, who owns what — especially in transcripts with multiple speakers.
How to address it
Proofreading is essential, but the goal is not to catch everything from scratch — it is to fix a few errors in an output that is already 80% right.
AI only sees text. It misses tone, body language, sarcasm, and what was said between the lines — including the things people implied but did not say outright. A meeting summary can be technically accurate and still miss the real outcome.
How to address it
Use the summary as a starting point: add your own interpretation of what was not.
For long documents, AI may silently drop large portions. This is more dangerous when you have never seen the document before and have no way to know what is missing.
How to address it
After reading a summary of an unfamiliar document, prompt AI with follow-up questions: "What else does this document cover that was not in the summary?" This surfaces what was left out.

Additional lifehacks

  • Split complex asks into separate prompts rather than combining them — ask for decisions, then actions, then owners.
  • For repetitive extraction, show one example output first, then ask AI to apply it across the rest.
Tradeoff
Speed vs. completeness

If you are already familiar with the subject or document, going straight to an AI summary is low risk — you are likely to notice anything important that is missing. If the subject and contents are unfamiliar, at least one person on the team should read through it first, then use AI to generate a condensed reference for others. This way only one person needs to read the document in full to validate completeness; the rest of the team gets the summary, and it is still faster to produce than writing it manually.

Writing text

The same statistical similarity that lets AI summarize also lets it generate prose, replicating how similar ideas are typically phrased. The same mechanism is also its biggest weakness: AI depends on filler and transitional phrases to move between thoughts that might not feel natural.

↗ Where this is most useful
  • Drafting emails, memos, and announcements from bullet points
  • Writing standard responses to recurring enquiries
  • Turning data or findings into a report narrative
  • Adapting the same message for different audiences or recipients (different content emphasis)

What AI handles vs. what you must own

AI
  • Converting bullet points or intent into fluent prose
  • Producing first drafts of standard formats at speed
  • Generating structural variations of the same message
Human
  • Providing the key facts, intent, and constraints — AI cannot invent these
  • Verifying every claim, rate, name, and date in the output
  • Deciding what to include or omit before AI writes anything
  • Final judgment on whether the output is appropriate to send

Limitations and how to address them

If an LLM "needs" to reference a fact or a number that is not available in the context, it is extremely likely to make one up to ensure the argument sounds coherent.
How to address it
Treat every specific fact in the output as unverified until you have checked it.
LLMs rely heavily on filler words and clichés when generating writing. These patterns are now commonly associated with AI and can create a reputational risk in client communications.
How to address it
Use system prompts to ban specific phrases (e.g., "it is worth noting", "in today's fast-paced", "I hope this finds you well"). Provide the LLM with a sample of your own writing as a style reference. In most cases you will still need a manual pass to make the output sound human.
First drafts from broad prompts are structurally sound but tonally flat; the language consistently outpaces the meaning.
How to address it
When possible, use bullet points or a clear purpose statement to generate the first draft rather than a broad prompt. For longer pieces, iterate between AI generation and manual edits. As you rewrite, evaluate for meaning.

Additional lifehacks

  • Create a document with your voice guidelines and phrases to avoid that you can upload to any LLM session.
  • For recurring formats, save the prompt as a reusable template; the prompt is the asset, not the output.
  • Do not regenerate without providing specific feedback on what to adjust.
  • Develop intuition for when to switch to manual edits rather than continuing to prompt.
Tradeoff
Speed vs. ownership of content

The faster you prompt, the less of the content is yours. A broad prompt produces fluent prose where AI has made the substantive decisions — what to include, what to emphasize, what facts to reference. For low-stakes content that is an acceptable shortcut. For anything where the facts, framing, or commitments matter, write the substance first and let AI handle the prose.

Transforming text

Transforming text combines reading for meaning and adapting style. Because AI is accounting for two targets simultaneously, it is inherently a more complex ask than summarizing or generating from scratch. It is among the most useful language processing applications, but also the most hit or miss.

↗ Where this is most useful
  • Rewriting a technical document in plain language for a non-technical audience
  • Converting an internal memo into a client-facing communication
  • Adapting a formal report into a Slack update or slide deck bullets
  • Adjusting the tone of an email — warmer, more formal, more direct
  • Checking copy against a brand voice guide before it goes out

What AI handles vs. what you must own

AI
  • Adapting register and formality to a described audience
  • Reformatting between structures (prose to bullets, bullets to prose)
  • Applying a defined style constraint across a piece of content
  • Generating multiple format variations from a single source
Human
  • Re-validating the transformed output — tone changes frequently shift meaning
  • Verifying the adapted version still says what you intended
  • Providing the style guide or voice standard
  • Deciding which version is right for which context

Limitations and how to address them

AI adapts text by balancing two targets: original meaning and new style. When the gap is large, it prioritizes one over the other, causing meaning loss or insufficient style adaptation.
How to address it
Use AI for templated or recurring communication where the gap is small. For anything dense with implicit meaning, adapt manually. Always double check if the meaning is preserved.
AI approximates style categories — formal, conversational, direct — not individual voice.
How to address it
Paste a sample of your own writing alongside the request and expect improvement rather than replication.

Additional lifehacks

  • Ask AI to first summarise your text as bullet points, confirm the bullets are accurate, then rewrite in the new tone using the bullets as an anchor. The bullets lock the content so the tone can change freely.
  • For multi-audience transformation in one pass: "Rewrite for three audiences: [A], [B], [C]. Keep the same facts. Adapt emphasis and register for each."
Tradeoff
Fluency vs. fidelity

For templated communication and frequently used formats, AI is effective at changing voice with minimal impact on content. However, when writing is dense with both implicit and explicit meanings, changing voice through AI will frequently cost at least partially losing the meaning.

Functionality Spotlight

A live exercise using a real Ampersand-style transcript. Download it, run it through Copilot twice, and see what changes when you give AI a frame to work with.

Exercise goal

Paste the same transcript into Copilot twice — once with no direction, once with a structured frame. The difference between the two outputs is the entire point. You do not need to understand the theory first. The comparison makes it obvious.

Questions to ask yourself
  • Which output could you act on immediately?
  • What did the vague prompt leave out that the directed one surfaced?
  • What would you have missed if you only ran the first prompt?
  • How long did it take to write the structured prompt vs. the value it added?
Sample transcript
Ampersand Q3 Campaign Planning Call
A realistic internal meeting covering decisions, open items, and a flagged risk — designed to show the difference between prompt types.
Download
Sample prompts to try

Paste the transcript into Copilot inside Teams (Recap tab) or Copilot Chat. Run Prompt 1 first. Save the output. Then run Prompt 2 and compare.

1 No direction
Copy prompt

Summarise this meeting transcript.

No frame. No priorities. Copilot decides what to include.

2 With direction
Copy prompt

Summarise this meeting transcript. I need three things: (1) decisions made and who owns them, (2) open items that still need follow-up with a named owner where possible, (3) anything flagged as a risk or blocker. Keep each section to 3 bullet points maximum. If something was discussed but not resolved, put it in open items — not decisions.

Structured frame. Copilot has no room to guess what matters.

Two things to keep in mind after you run this

  • A transcript summary can be technically accurate and still miss the real outcome of a meeting. AI only sees text — it cannot capture tone, what was implied, or decisions made informally after the call. Use the summary as a starting point, not as the record.
  • If a document is unfamiliar, someone on the team should read it before relying on an AI summary. AI may silently drop large portions of a long document and will not flag what is missing. One person reads to validate; everyone else gets the summary.
