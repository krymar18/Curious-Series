Introduction to Agentic Workflows

In Categories 1–4, AI responds to one prompt and you review the output before anything happens. Agentic AI takes sequences of actions — it acts first, and you review the result. Users will encounter this whether or not they seek it out. This section gives you a mental model before you encounter it unexpectedly — because the failure modes are different, and harder to catch.

Objective

Understand what agentic AI is, how it differs from prompt-response, and how to design oversight into workflows before they run — not after they produce a bad result.

Core idea

The Timing of Oversight

The single most important concept in this category — and the one that changes everything else.

The difference between prompt-response and agentic AI is not capability — it is timing. In prompt-response, you review before anything happens. In agentic, AI acts first. The same properties that make AI fast and fluent and the same weaknesses that make it unreliable are present in both modes. What changes is the consequence of trusting an AI error — in agentic workflows, that error may have already propagated through five steps before you see it.

What Agentic Means — and How It Differs

The conceptual foundation for this category. Agentic AI takes sequences of actions — it makes intermediate decisions and acts on them, rather than producing one output for you to review. The core distinction is timing: in prompt-response, you review before anything happens; in agentic, AI acts first.

↗ Where this is most useful
  • Recognising when a Copilot feature is acting agentically vs. responding to a single prompt
  • Understanding what "approve before sending" controls in Copilot agents actually protect against
  • Identifying which AI tasks in your workflow involve autonomous action vs. human review before action
  • Deciding whether a given task is appropriate to delegate to an agent or should stay as prompt-response

Prompt-response vs. agentic

Prompt-response (Categories 1–4)
  • Human reviews before anything happens — lower risk, more oversight
  • Slower for high-volume tasks — each step requires human input
  • Errors are caught before they have consequences
  • Better when tasks require judgment or vary significantly
Agentic (Category 5)
  • AI acts before human reviews — higher efficiency, higher risk
  • Faster for well-defined, high-volume, repeatable tasks
  • Errors can propagate through multiple steps before detection
  • Better when tasks have clear rules and low variability

Limitations and how to address them

Users underestimate how often agentic behaviour is already happening. A flow routing a document, an AI categorising a ticket — these are all agentic.
How to address it
Identify which AI tools in your current workflow act before you review, and which produce output for you to review first. That distinction changes what oversight you need.
The habits that make someone a good prompt writer — iterate, refine, regenerate — do not address agentic failure modes.
How to address it
For agentic workflows, the design work happens before the workflow runs — not after it produces a bad result. Define the rules, scope, and checkpoints upfront.
Prompt Chaining — The Manual Bridge

Using the output of one AI prompt as the input to the next — building a multi-step workflow manually, one step at a time. This is the bridge between single-prompt use and full agent delegation. It keeps the human in the loop between every step by design: slower than a full agent, but errors are caught at each handoff rather than at the end.

↗ Where this is most useful
  • Using a meeting summary as the input for a follow-up email draft
  • Using an analysis output as the input for a client narrative
  • Using extracted action items as the input for individual task assignments
  • Using a brainstorm output as the input for a structured proposal section
  • Building a multi-step research and synthesis workflow with human review at each step

What AI handles vs. what you must own

AI
  • Carrying output from one step to the next
  • Maintaining context across a multi-step task
  • Producing intermediate outputs that would be tedious to create manually
Human
  • Reviewing the output at each step before using it as the next input — this is the main safety advantage of chaining
  • Designing the sequence — AI does not plan the chain, you do
  • Deciding when to correct, accept, or restart a chain

Limitations and how to address them

A small error in step one compounds through all subsequent steps. The later in the chain an error is caught, the more expensive it is to correct.
How to address it
Name your steps explicitly — "Step 1 of 3: summarise. Step 2: draft. Step 3: transform." This makes it easier to identify where in the chain something went wrong.
Context degrades across a long chain. AI does not carry memory between separate prompts — you must explicitly pass the relevant context forward at each step.
How to address it
At each step, paste in only what the next step needs — not the entire history. This keeps the context clean and the output more precise.
Prompt chaining feels controlled because the human is involved between steps. But if each review is too fast or too shallow, the control is illusory.
How to address it
For any step that involves a commitment, a live system, or content going to an external party — slow down. The review at that step matters more than the others.
Tradeoff
Chaining with minimal review vs. chaining with review at every step

Moving quickly through a chain is acceptable when every step is low-risk and the final output is fully reviewed before use — but an error from step one propagates silently, so only use this approach when every step is reversible. Reviewing at each step is slower but far safer: errors are caught where they originate. This is required for any step involving a commitment, live system, or external-facing content.

Agentic AI in Practice — What Users Will Encounter

Concrete examples of agentic behaviour in tools already in or adjacent to Ampersand's environment. Research and synthesis at scale sits here: when AI autonomously retrieves, evaluates, and combines information across multiple sources, it is agentic — multiple retrieval steps, intermediate judgments, compounding failure modes.

Copilot agents and actions: Copilot Studio custom agents, Copilot actions in Teams and Outlook — the most immediately relevant agentic tools for Ampersand staff.

↗ Where this is most useful
  • A Copilot agent that monitors a SharePoint folder and routes new files based on content
  • Copilot in Outlook drafting responses to a category of inbound emails — with human review before sending
  • An AI research workflow that retrieves, reads, and synthesises information across multiple sources
  • A Copilot Studio agent handling first-response to a defined category of internal requests
  • A Power Automate flow with an AI classification step that routes inbound orders by type
  • An AI monitoring workflow that flags orders where spend falls below contracted threshold

Limitations and how to address them

For AI-powered research: the output is a starting point, not a conclusion. AI retrieves and synthesises, but it cannot verify the accuracy of the sources it finds.
How to address it
Verify key claims against the original sources before using research output to inform a decision or share with a client. Ask AI to flag the confidence level and recency of each source.
Agents do not announce when they are uncertain or have made an assumption. They continue acting. Silence is not confirmation.
How to address it
Build logging into any agentic workflow so you can audit what actions were taken. Review the first several runs manually before reducing oversight.
Scope creep: agents given broad instructions will take broad actions.
How to address it
Define scope tightly. Start with the narrowest possible task definition, validate it works, then expand. Never give an agent more authority than the task requires.
Tradeoff
When agentic is appropriate vs. when to stay with prompt-response

Agentic AI is appropriate for high-volume, well-defined tasks with reversible actions that have been manually validated and run consistently before being automated — where the failure mode is visible and correctable before downstream consequences. Stay with prompt-response for high variability, judgment requirements, or relationship-specific context; anything where an error is irreversible or has external consequences; and any new or unfamiliar task — understand the decision logic manually before delegating it.

Where Agents Fail

The failure modes unique to agentic systems. Understanding these is more important than understanding the value case — because agentic failures are harder to catch and more expensive to reverse than anything in Categories 1–4.

↗ Where this is most useful
  • Identifying which repetitive workflows have conditions that make them good agent candidates
  • Recognising early warning signs that an agent is producing errors before they propagate
  • Designing a manual version of a workflow before automating it — to understand the decision points that need explicit rules
  • Auditing an existing automated workflow for insufficient human oversight

Failure modes and how to address them

Cascading errors: a wrong decision in step 2 of a 10-step workflow propagates silently through steps 3–10. This is the defining failure mode of agentic systems.
How to address it
Design workflows so irreversible actions are the last step and require explicit human confirmation where possible. The earlier in the sequence a human reviews, the cheaper the correction.
Silent failures: agents do not announce when they have made an assumption or hit an edge case.
How to address it
Build logging into every agentic workflow. A log tells you what happened after the fact — use it to audit, debug, and reverse where possible.
Poorly defined instructions produce a workflow that consistently produces bad outputs — not just one bad output.
How to address it
Test the workflow on a small controlled batch before scaling. Run the first several instances manually in parallel to verify the agent is making the right decisions.
Human Oversight and Checkpoints

What meaningful oversight looks like in agentic workflows. Not just approving the final output — designing checkpoints into the workflow itself where a human reviews before the next consequential step executes.

↗ Where this is most useful
  • Designing a Copilot agent workflow with an explicit human approval step before any external communication is sent
  • Setting up a review checkpoint after an AI classification step, before items are routed
  • Running the first 10–20 instances of a new agent workflow manually in parallel
  • Building an audit log so every action can be reviewed after the fact
  • Defining escalation rules: what conditions cause the agent to stop and request human review

Limitations and how to address them

Oversight designed only at the end of a multi-step agent is almost no oversight. All the intermediate decisions have already been made.
How to address it
Place checkpoints at the step just before the most consequential action — not after it. The earlier the checkpoint, the cheaper the correction.
Human approval steps that are too easy to click through are not oversight. If the reviewer cannot evaluate what the agent did, the checkpoint is cosmetic.
How to address it
Design checkpoints to show the reviewer exactly what the agent decided and why — not just the output. Make it possible to reject and redirect, not just approve.

What the human must never delegate

  • Sending any external communication without a human review step
  • Modifying or deleting records in live systems without a reversibility mechanism
  • Making financial commitments, approvals, or authorisations autonomously
  • Any action where an error cannot be easily reversed within your normal operational window
  • Scaling an agentic workflow before running at least 10–20 instances manually in parallel

Conclusion & Reflections

Take a moment to reflect — it's what makes the learning stick.

Key Takeaways
  • The difference between prompt-response and agentic AI is timing, not capability. In agentic, AI acts before you review.
  • The habits that make someone good at prompting do not address agentic failure modes. Design the rules upfront.
  • In prompt chaining, the control is only real if you actually review at each step. Fast review is illusory control.
  • Silence is not confirmation. Agents do not announce when they have made an assumption — build logging into every agentic workflow.
  • Start with the narrowest scope. Validate on 10–20 real instances before reducing oversight.
  • Never delegate: external communications without review, live system modifications without reversibility, or financial commitments autonomously.
Reflection
  • Think of a repetitive task you do regularly. If an agent made one wrong decision every twenty runs, would you catch it? When?
  • Have you ever clicked through an approval step without reading what you were approving? What was the consequence?
  • Which AI tools you currently use are acting before you review, rather than waiting for your approval?
  • What would you need to see in a log to trust that an automated workflow ran correctly?
