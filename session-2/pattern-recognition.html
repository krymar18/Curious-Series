Pattern Recognition and Analysis

Objective

Use AI as a fast first-pass analyst — surfacing patterns, themes, and anomalies for human judgment, not as a substitute for it.

Rationale

LLMs are pattern machines — the same capability that makes them useful for language tasks can be applied to enhance analysis. Give AI a large volume of text, data, or communications and it will surface patterns faster than any individual could. However, identifying a pattern and judging its importance are different things; when using AI for analysis, there is always a risk of missing the most significant pattern relative to the objective. AI is best used to enhance analysis, not replace the analyst.

Two Parts Of Any Analysis

Searching by Meaning

FGood analysis starts by collecting relevant information and isolating only the factors that matter, followed by comparing isolated data to draw conclusions. This section covers using AI to collect more relevant information through semantic search. In doing so we take advantage of three sources: LLMs' general knowledge from training data, their ability to conduct internet searches, and semantic search of company SharePoints in the case of Microsoft Copilot

Technology link — optional reading

When you search by meaning, AI converts your query into a vector — a mathematical representation of meaning — and finds the content where those vectors are closest. This is why 'what happens if we cancel?' finds the termination clause even though the word 'termination' does not appear in your query. It also explains why rephrasing a question changes the results: the vector shifts, and the nearest content shifts with it. Documents are indexed in advance, which is why newly uploaded files may not be immediately searchable. Copilot in SharePoint uses a version of this mechanism alongside keyword and graph-based retrieval.

↗ Where this is most useful
  • Finding the answer to a specific question in a long contract or policy document
  • Locating a document on SharePoint when you remember the topic but not the title
  • Searching across multiple documents to find which ones address a specific topic
  • Asking questions of an SOP or process guide without reading every step
  • Finding what was said about a specific topic across a set of meeting transcripts
  • Locating previous proposals or decisions on a given subject

What AI handles vs. what you must own

AI
  • Searching for content by meaning across a document or collection
  • Returning the most relevant passages for a given query
  • Answering specific questions from within a document's content
Human
  • Verifying any high-stakes answer against the original source
  • Deciding whether the retrieved content actually answers your question or is merely adjacent
  • Providing the document — AI cannot access systems it has not been given

Limitations and how to address them

AI may answer confidently from the wrong section of a document — or generate a plausible answer that is not actually in the text at all.
How to address it
Ask AI to quote the relevant passage alongside its answer. If it cannot quote it, the answer may not exist in the document. For high-stakes questions, locate and read the original passage yourself.
Retrieval is sensitive to phrasing. The same question worded differently may return different passages — or miss the right one entirely.
How to address it
If the answer seems off, rephrase the question rather than regenerating. Plain language works better than trying to match the document's vocabulary — "what happens if we cancel?" outperforms "termination clause language".
Searching across many documents simultaneously reduces precision. The more sources, the more the answer becomes a blend rather than a direct quote.
How to address it
For high-stakes multi-document searches, follow up with "which specific document does this answer come from?" and verify in that document directly.
Tradeoff
Fast directional answer vs. verified answer

For low-stakes lookups, ask a broad question and accept that the answer may be a paraphrase rather than a direct quote. For contractual or compliance questions, ask AI to quote the passage — this adds one step but makes verification immediate — and always read the original passage yourself before acting.

Analysis and Finding Insights

Asking AI to find what is notable, what repeats, what is out of range, or what the data suggests. AI is a fast first-pass analyst — useful for surfacing candidates for human judgment. The human decides whether the insight is real and whether it matters.

For large or sensitive datasets: ask AI to write the analysis code (Python, SQL, Excel) rather than pasting the data. Share only column headers and a description. This keeps the data private and produces more reliable results at scale.

↗ Where this is most useful
  • Finding the most common themes or patterns across a large set of text responses, call notes, or feedback
  • Identifying outliers and anomalies within a clean dataset
  • Comparing performance across markets, accounts, or segments to surface what stands out
  • Ranking findings by frequency or magnitude to prioritise where to focus
  • Identifying which variables tend to appear together across a set of records

What AI handles vs. what you must own

AI
  • Identifying recurring themes and patterns across large volumes of text or data
  • Flagging outliers and values outside the expected range
  • Ranking findings by frequency or magnitude
Human
  • Drawing and validating the conclusion
  • Attributing correlation or causation to the trend
  • Defining what normal looks like before asking AI to find anomalies
  • Evaluating output for hallucinations and bias

Limitations and how to address them

AI will find a pattern in almost any dataset, including random ones. A confident presentation of a pattern does not mean the pattern is real or meaningful.
How to address it
After AI surfaces a pattern, ask: "What alternative explanation could produce the same result?" This is the fastest check against treating coincidence as signal.
The larger the dataset, the shallower the analysis. AI loses analytical precision at scale — treat large-dataset insights as directional only.
How to address it
For large or sensitive datasets: share only column headers and a description, ask AI to write the analysis code, and run it locally. More accurate, more repeatable, data stays private.
AI cannot distinguish correlation from causation. It will describe the relationship fluently without knowing whether one thing caused the other.
How to address it
When AI identifies a relationship between two variables, ask explicitly: "Is there a plausible causal mechanism here, or could this be coincidental?" It will reason about it more explicitly if you ask.
Anomaly detection without a defined baseline is unreliable. AI will define "normal" itself, which may not match your actual operational baseline.
How to address it
Define the baseline explicitly in your prompt — "flag anything more than 15% above or below the 90-day average" is more useful than "find the anomalies".

Additional lifehacks

  • Ask AI to group AND count — "group these 50 responses by theme and count how many fall into each group" produces more actionable output than "what are the themes?"
  • Ask for findings by severity or frequency — it forces a ranking that makes the output immediately usable.
Tradeoff
Fast directional insight vs. reliable, repeatable analysis

Pasting a manageable dataset and asking for the top patterns is good for orientation — small datasets produce stronger signal and you can act quickly. For larger or recurring analysis, ask AI to write the analysis code and run it locally: more accurate, keeps data private, and you can rerun it as data changes. Always define "normal" explicitly before asking for anomalies rather than letting AI choose its own baseline.

Exercise goal

Ask Copilot to find patterns without any baseline definition — then ask again with an explicit one. Without a baseline, Copilot sets its own definition of normal, which may not match your operational reality. Questions to ask yourself
  • What did Copilot define as "normal" when you gave it no baseline?
  • Did the undirected output flag the same anomalies as the directed one?
  • For each anomaly surfaced — what is the most innocent explanation?
  • Which findings are worth investigating and which are probably noise?
