Feedback, Iteration & Memory

How AI learns from feedback, how to get better outputs through iteration, and how Copilot remembers what matters across sessions.

Objective

Understand the difference between model-level feedback and in-session iteration, develop a practical feedback habit, and learn how to use Copilot's memory features to carry your preferences forward.

Key idea

Your first prompt is a draft, not an order. The skill isn't writing a perfect prompt — it's knowing how to steer from where you land to where you want to be.

Theory

How AI learns from feedback

There are two kinds of feedback in AI tools. They look similar but do completely different things.

Thumbs up / thumbs down — model-level feedback. The thumbs buttons in Copilot, ChatGPT, and Claude feed into a training process called Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). When you thumbs-down a response, you're not fixing that answer — you're contributing a data point that helps shape how future versions of the model respond to everyone. Think of it like a restaurant comment card. The kitchen doesn't remake your meal because you filled it out. But if enough people flag the same dish, it eventually changes. Use the thumbs buttons deliberately — they cost nothing and matter at scale.

What thumbs down actually does

Your feedback is aggregated with thousands of others. A reward model learns from these preferences and is used to fine-tune the AI's behavior in future training runs. Your specific conversation is not reprocessed — the effect is gradual and collective, not immediate and individual.

In-session iteration — the skill that changes your results today. Within a conversation, every message you send updates the AI's context. It sees your original request, its response, and your correction — and adjusts. The more specific your correction, the better the adjustment. Most people abandon a conversation when the first output is wrong and start over. That's the most common mistake. Starting over erases all context. Iterating builds on it.

Stay in the conversation
  • The AI already knows your task, your context, and what it tried.
  • Your correction steers from a known starting point.
  • Each round gets faster — you're narrowing, not starting over.
Don't start a new chat
  • Opening a new chat loses all shared context.
  • You'll repeat yourself and get a similar first output.
  • The problem wasn't the prompt — it was the missing correction.
In-session iteration

Giving AI good feedback is a specific skill. Vague reactions produce vague adjustments.

The feedback sequence. When an output isn't right, work through this order rather than sending one big correction: structure first (is the format wrong?), content second (is it missing something or going the wrong direction?), tone and voice last. Tone corrections on a badly structured response are wasted.

The AI doesn't get defensive

You can say "this is corporate garbage, rewrite it as plain English" and it will. Directness works better than politely asking it to "improve" something.

Specific — works
  • "The reader is a non-technical sales manager. Rewrite for them."
  • "Cut this to 3 sentences. Nothing else."
  • "The second paragraph is redundant — remove it."
  • "Here's an email I like: [paste]. Match that tone."
  • "Stop using the word 'leverage.' Replace every instance."
Vague — doesn't work
  • "Make it better."
  • "Improve the tone."
  • "This isn't quite right."
  • "Make it more professional."
  • "I don't like it — try again."

Lifehacks.

  • Ask for options first. "Give me three different openings." Choosing is faster than correcting.
  • Tell it what to keep. "The second paragraph is perfect. Only rewrite the first and third."
  • Use contrast. "Version A is too formal, version B is too casual. Find the middle."
  • Name the problem, not the solution. "This sounds like it was written by a committee" is more useful than "make it more personal."
  • Ask it to critique its own output. "What's weak about this response?" Then use that as your next prompt.
Copilot memory and cross-chat features

Every session starts from zero by default. Copilot's memory features change that — but only if you use them deliberately.

What it can remember
  • Preferences you state explicitly — "I prefer bullet points" or "keep emails under 5 lines."
  • Working style patterns inferred from your conversations over time.
  • Recurring topics and project context.
  • For premium users: context from Teams, Outlook, and meetings via Microsoft Graph.
What it won't remember
  • Every detail from every chat — only what it judges useful to retain.
  • Anything from chats you've deleted.
  • Anything if Chat History personalization is turned off.
  • Specific facts mentioned once in passing — it remembers patterns, not transcripts.

Don't wait for Copilot to infer your preferences — tell it directly.

  • "Please remember I write for non-technical audiences and prefer plain language."
  • "Remember I'm in Traffic & Ops at a multiscreen TV advertising company."
  • "Remember I prefer bullet points unless I ask for prose."

How to manage memory

Memory is on by default for premium users. To manage: Copilot Chat → Settings → Personalization → Chat history. Turning it off deletes all inferences within 30 days. Your IT admin may have adjusted this at the org level — if memory features aren't visible, check before the next session.

Cross-chat context (premium). Copilot can draw from Teams meetings, Outlook threads, and SharePoint documents when relevant. Useful — but be deliberate about what you ask it to reference, especially anything involving confidential client or legal content.

The retrieval prompt

Every correction you make in a session reveals something about your preferences. The retrieval prompt collects all of that and writes it up as a reusable reference. Run it at the end of any session where you did meaningful iteration.

Retrieval prompt

Review our conversation so far. Identify all the corrections, preferences, and instructions I gave you — explicitly or implicitly. Rewrite them into a single reusable document describing my writing preferences: tone, format, what I kept correcting, and what I responded well to. Write it as a short reference I can paste at the start of a future session to get to this quality of output immediately.

If the output is too generic, follow up with:

Follow-up if output is vague

This is too generic. Go back through our conversation and pull out the specific corrections I made — exact things I rejected, exact language I preferred, specific structural choices I pushed for. Make it concrete enough that someone who has never talked to me could match my style.

Building a personal prompt library

A prompt library is a collection of prompts, context blocks, and preference documents you paste at the start of sessions to skip setup. Instead of re-explaining your role and tone preferences every time, you paste your library entry and start at full speed.

What to include
  • Your writing preferences document from the retrieval prompt.
  • A persona block — your role, team, and typical audience.
  • Standing constraints — things you never want in your outputs.
  • Task-specific prompts you've refined and reuse often.
How to maintain it
  • After any productive session, run the retrieval prompt and update your document.
  • Keep it somewhere you can copy from quickly — OneNote, Word, or a pinned Teams message.
  • Trim it periodically — a 500-word preamble slows you down as much as it helps.
  • Version it. Your Week 1 entry will look different from your Week 4 entry.
Starter template — your first library entry
## Who I am
[Your role, team, and company context. Who you work with and who you typically write for.]

## My writing preferences
[Paste your retrieval prompt output here.]

## Standing constraints
[Things you never want in your outputs — e.g. no bullet points in client emails, no jargon, always include a clear next step, never exceed X words.]

## This session
[What you're working on today — task, audience, goal.]

Practice Activity — Full Iteration Loop

Put everything from this section together in one exercise. You'll iterate on a rewrite, run the retrieval prompt, and end with a usable prompt library entry.

The exercise

Work through these steps in order inside a single Copilot conversation. Don't start a new chat between steps.

The text

"In today's fast-paced and ever-evolving business landscape, it is of paramount importance that organizations leverage synergistic frameworks to holistically optimize cross-functional stakeholder engagement and drive scalable value creation across all verticals."

  • Step 1 — First pass. Send this to Copilot with no context. Just paste the text and ask it to rewrite. Look at what you get.
  • Step 2 — Add context. Tell it who will read this and what the sentence actually needs to say. Ask it to try again.
  • Step 3 — Get specific. Name exactly one thing wrong with the current version. Don't say "make it better" — say what specifically doesn't work.
  • Step 4 — Tone reference. Paste a sentence or two of writing you like and ask it to match that tone.
  • Step 5 — Constrain it. "Cut this to one sentence under 20 words."
  • Step 6 — Run the retrieval prompt from section 4.4. Save the output.
  • Step 7 — Fill in the starter template from section 4.5 using what you just generated.

What to notice

Compare Step 1 output to Step 5 output. The difference isn't a better prompt — it's a better feedback loop. Your retrieval document captures exactly what you did to get there.

Debrief questions
  • At which step did the output make the biggest jump? What did you do differently at that point?
  • Did Copilot ever push back on your feedback or ask a clarifying question? What triggered it?
  • Is the retrieval prompt output specific enough to be useful, or is it still generic? What would you add?

Conclusion & Reflections

Take a moment to reflect — it's what makes the learning stick.

Take aways
  • Thumbs up/down shapes the model over time. In-session iteration changes your output right now.
  • Stay in the conversation. Starting over erases context and resets your progress.
  • Good feedback names the problem — structure, content, or tone — not just the feeling that something is off.
  • Copilot Memory works best when you tell it what to remember explicitly, not when you hope it infers it.
  • The retrieval prompt converts your feedback history into a reusable document — run it at the end of any productive session.
  • Your prompt library compounds. Each session that ends with a retrieval prompt makes the next one faster.
Reflection
  • When you got a bad output this week, did you iterate or start over? What would have changed if you'd stayed in the conversation?
  • Did Copilot's memory carry anything forward from a previous session? Was it useful or surprising?
  • After running the retrieval prompt, did the output match how you actually think about your own writing preferences? What was missing?
  • What's one correction you keep making to AI outputs that you haven't written down yet?
  • Looking at the Ampersand spotlight table — what would you add or change for your specific role?
